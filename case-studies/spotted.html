Turning Swiggy into something you do with friends

Ordering in is one of the most social meals we eat, and it happens completely alone. I designed Spotted, a new Swiggy tab where friends see each other's orders live, roast them, copy them, and choose food from each other's photos.

The feature in four taps

  1. See who's ordering. Friends' live orders sit at the top as rings with a countdown to their doorbell.
  2. Roast them. Six stickers, no free text, only while the food is on its way. It lands on their phone as a notification.
  3. Order the same. One tap copies a friend's order, and they find out they were copied.
  4. Choose from friends' photos. A ring on a restaurant opens friends' Instants, newest first. No strangers, no star ratings.

The problem

People already turn food delivery into a social thing, just not inside the app. They screenshot orders into group chats, tease the friend on their third biryani of the week, and ask "who's ordering?" before anyone opens Swiggy. All of that happens on WhatsApp and Instagram, so Swiggy sees none of it.

Two other gaps sit right next to that. Swiggy's re-engagement notifications ("Are you missing us?") are generic, and generic pushes get opened about 4% of the time. And restaurant ratings come from strangers, as an average that tells you little about how the food actually arrives.

The question I set myself: can the same friend banter bring people back into the app, start orders, and replace strangers' reviews?

Design decisions

Permission first, three questions at a time

Spotted is off until you turn it on. Three separate choices: what friends see (that you're ordering, the restaurant, or the dish), who sees it, and who can send stickers. Your address and the rider's location are never shared, and Ghost mode hides you in one tap.

What you eat is personal. Trust has to come before the fun.

Stickers instead of a comment box

Six fixed stickers in Gen Z slang ("Eating alone? The audacity", "Bite tax. Pay up.") and no free text. They only work while an order is on the way, so every roast lands at the funniest moment and expires with the delivery.

Free text turns into review wars. Stickers keep it playful.

Put "Order the same" next to the roast

The moment a sticker makes someone hungry is the moment to order. The button sits on every live order, and the friend who got copied is told, which starts the loop again.

The feature has to pay for itself in orders, not scrolling.

Instants: live camera, packaging only, friends only

After delivery you can snap the food the way it came, add a quick caption ("Too good. Delicious", or an honest "Never ordering from here") and pick which friends get it. Gallery uploads are off and abusive captions are blocked. Instants then show as story rings on the restaurant in the normal menu.

Happy eaters rarely write reviews, but they love showing off good food.

Rewards that build the habit

3 Instants unlock ₹50 off, 5 unlock a week of free delivery. Only Instants sent to friends count, so nobody is paid to praise a restaurant to strangers.

Reward the behaviour, not the opinion.

What I deliberately left out

Public profiles, follower counts, influencer leaderboards, star ratings and plated food photos. Each one would make Spotted another feed people perform on.

It only stays fun while it stays between friends.

Designing the prototype page itself

Let people try it before they read about it

Visitors decide whether to stay within about 10 seconds, so the page opens on two linked phones and a 4-tap guided try-out instead of a paragraph.

Show the aha before the essay. NN/g

4 steps, a hint, and no dark overlay

Chameleon's data shows 3-step tours are finished 66% of the time and completion falls below half after 5. NN/g found tutorials and coach-mark overlays made apps feel harder without helping anyone succeed. So the tour is 4 taps with one pulsing green hint on the next thing to tap.

Guide the next tap, not the whole screen. NN/g

One phone that hands over on mobile

Most LinkedIn visits come from phones, where two side-by-side phones were unreadable. On a small screen there's one full-size phone that switches to your friend's phone with a "Meanwhile, on Vivek's phone" banner.

The cause and the effect both have to be readable.

How Swiggy could prove it, and what's next

A 4-week test. Give Spotted to whole friend groups picked at random rather than to individual users, the same design as the Aral and Walker trial, so people in the test don't roast friends outside it. The deciding number is orders per user per week. Alongside it: sticker open rate, the share of live orders that get an "Order the same", and Instants per delivered order. Stop if notification opt-outs, uninstalls or switches to Ghost mode rise.

What I'd do next: run five quick interviews on what people would and wouldn't share about their food, test the sticker copy for tone across age groups, and design the empty state for someone whose friends aren't on Spotted yet, which is where most people would start.
